## Book: The Metamorphosis

Franz Kafka’s *The Metamorphosis* (1915) begins with Gregor Samsa waking in an insect-like body. He has supported his parents and sister through a job he dislikes. His transformation leaves him unable to provide for them, and the story follows his increasing exclusion from family life.

One of Gregor’s first concerns is getting to work:

> my train leaves at five.

That response shows how strongly work governs his understanding of himself. Even a change he cannot explain becomes a problem of lateness and disappointing others. His employer sends the chief clerk to demand an explanation that morning. His mother defends him:

> The lad only ever thinks about the business.

She also worries that he rarely goes out. Her defence gives us a sense of how much of his life work occupies.

At home, his sister Grete initially brings him food and cleans his room. Her care gradually gives way to impatience and rejection. Gregor still feels responsibility and affection, while others increasingly regard him as a burden. Kafka examines what happens to someone’s place in a family when he can no longer fulfil his role as provider.

When Grete plays the violin, the narration follows Gregor’s response:

> Was he an animal if music could captivate him so?

His pleasure in her playing gives the reader access to feelings his family increasingly fails to recognize.

## Film: The Truman Show

Peter Weir’s *The Truman Show* (1998) follows a man whose ordinary life has been arranged for television. Truman Burbank does not know that his town is a set, or that his wife and friends are actors. He trusts them because they belong to the only world he has known.

The film’s central concern is how someone can recognize and question a life controlled by others. The production supplies both Truman’s experiences and the people who help him interpret them. Its influence reaches into what he considers normal, whom he believes, and which possibilities seem available to him.

Christof, the television programme’s creator, explains Truman’s acceptance during an interview within the film:

> We accept the reality of the world with which we’re presented.

An early incident makes this control briefly visible: his car radio picks up instructions from the production team. What seemed to be an ordinary journey has been coordinated around him. The significance of such moments is that they give Truman reasons to doubt familiar explanations. Making choices of his own first requires him to question the account of his life that the production has taught him to accept.
