## Version A: History first

In nineteenth-century French painting, the naked male body often belonged to a god, a hero, or the ancient world. Caillebotte gave it a different occupation: scraping a floor. His three men bend over their tools, with wood shavings gathering around their hands. Their bodies belong to a working day. [1]

Painted in 1875, *The Floor Scrapers* gave unusual prominence to urban workers. Peasant labor already had an established place in French painting. Men working inside the modern city were less familiar subjects. When Caillebotte exhibited the picture with the Impressionists in 1876, it drew both admiration and criticism. The ordinary work represented here had become a dispute over what deserved to be painted. [2][3]

Look at how carefully he organizes that work. The floorboards stretch toward the window, pulling our eyes through the room. The men's bent backs interrupt those straight lines. Light picks out shoulders, forearms, and the pale strips emerging beneath their tools. The room takes shape through a series of repeated movements.

Its decorative walls and iron balcony suggest comfort. Below them, the men kneel among curls of wood. This puts two aspects of domestic life together: the elegance of an interior and the physical skill that produces it.

His own position complicates the scene. Family wealth from the textile business allowed him to paint without depending on sales. He looked at manual work from a life protected from that necessity. [3] That distance matters when we read the picture as an account of working life. We see the men's actions closely; their thoughts and circumstances remain outside the frame.

The painting can nevertheless change where we place our attention. A floor usually serves as the background for whatever happens above it. Here, the people making its surface occupy the center. The shavings have not yet been cleared away. The room still shows what its elegance requires.

## Version B: Scene first

The wood shavings are still on the floor. They collect beside a hand, curl beside the tools, and scatter across the light. Three men work on their knees. The central figure reaches forward with both arms, his hands gathered around a tool. Along the right wall, a bottle and glass sit within reach.

It is a room caught before the work has disappeared into its result. Once the shavings are swept away, a visitor could admire the floor without thinking about these movements. Caillebotte keeps the movements visible. The long boards guide our eyes past shoulders, hands, and tools before they reach the window.

Painted in 1875, *The Floor Scrapers* brought unusual attention to workers inside the modern city. French artists had already made peasant labor a serious subject. Urban trades had received less attention. Caillebotte carried them into the Impressionists' exhibition of 1876, where the painting attracted praise and criticism. [2][3]

The bare torsos help explain the tension. Academic painting had long made room for idealized male bodies in ancient or heroic settings. Here, exposed skin belongs to an ordinary task. [1] The central man stretches his arms along the boards, pressing both hands around his tool. His lowered head gives us little access to his expression.

Around him, decorated walls and an ornate balcony establish the character of the room. Its comfort depends on people who must kneel to make it. Their skill is visible in the narrow strips they have worked, each running beside the next.

Caillebotte approached that relationship from a privileged position. His family's textile fortune freed him from needing to earn a living through painting. [3] His attention to these men does not erase the distance between their work and his.

The bottle and glass suggest a pause. We do not know when the men will take it, or what they might discuss. The painting brings us close enough to follow their hands across the boards. Their lives extend beyond what this room allows us to see.

### Differences between the sources

The older Orsay collection essay says there is no social, moralizing, or political message. The newer exhibition text offers a political reading of work and masculinity. The drafts discuss a social relationship visible in the painting without claiming a settled political intention.

The NGA brochure and Orsay collection essay state that the 1875 Salon rejected the painting. The newer Orsay exhibition text says it was likely rejected. Both drafts omit this disputed degree of certainty because it is unnecessary to their argument.

The workers' partial nudity does not establish their feelings or identities. The painting is an artistic representation, not an ethnographic interview or a complete record of working conditions.
