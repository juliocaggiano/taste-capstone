## The Potato Eaters — Vincent van Gogh, 1885

### A

In 1885, while living in the Dutch village of Nuenen, Vincent van Gogh was preparing a painting of rural workers. He spent the winter studying heads and hands, then assembled a scene of five people sharing a meal by lamplight.

The hands reaching toward the potatoes explain his choice of subject. He wanted viewers to connect them with the work of digging the soil. Writing to his brother Theo, he said the painting should show that its subjects “have thus honestly earned their food.” Their meal gives physical labour a place in the picture even though the working day is over.

The dark earth colours, coarse faces and prominent knuckles belong to the same argument. Van Gogh wanted the figures to retain the signs of the life they led. Smoothing their features or dressing them more elegantly would have weakened the connection he was trying to establish.

He admired peasant life as an alternative to the urban world he and Theo knew. He hoped this deliberately rough painting would find an audience through his brother, an art dealer in Paris.

### B

Five people gather around a dish of potatoes in Vincent van Gogh’s *The Potato Eaters* (1885). Their faces and hands receive much of the lamplight. The hands look suited to digging and lifting, and Van Gogh wanted their appearance to connect the food on the table with the labour that produced it.

He painted the work in Nuenen after months of studies, constructing much of the final scene from memory. The rough treatment was a considered choice. In his letters, he argued that giving rural workers polished features and fashionable clothes would misrepresent what he valued in their lives.

This places the painting within a nineteenth-century interest in peasant labour. Van Gogh admired Jean-François Millet and wanted to make ordinary country people the subject of serious art. The modest meal allows him to suggest both material hardship and his respect for work done by hand.

His admiration also shaped what he selected. He considered rural life better in several respects than the urban world he knew. The picture expresses that belief through the worn bodies and plain food.

## All Quiet on the Western Front — Erich Maria Remarque, 1929

### A

Erich Maria Remarque’s *All Quiet on the Western Front* (1929) follows Paul Bäumer, a young German soldier during the First World War. He and his schoolmates enlist after their teacher presents service as a patriotic duty. At the front, that promise gives way to hunger, bombardment and the effort to keep one another alive.

Paul learns to judge people by whether they can help him survive. His comrades provide food, practical knowledge and affection that official speeches cannot offer. But becoming an experienced soldier also separates him from the life he expected to lead.

On leave, he finds civilians confidently discussing how the war should be fought. They dismiss his experience when it contradicts their opinions. Back in his room, he tries to read the books that once absorbed him, hoping to recover his earlier interests. He cannot. As he explains:

> I find I do not belong here any more.

The damage is already present while Paul is alive and temporarily safe. War has interrupted his entry into adult life and replaced it with skills for enduring danger. He can rely on his comrades, but he no longer knows how the person he has become could return to the future imagined at school.

### B

In Erich Maria Remarque’s *All Quiet on the Western Front* (1929), Paul Bäumer and his German schoolmates enter the First World War encouraged by patriotic teaching. Their teachers speak of duty and glory; the soldiers become preoccupied with food, shelter and keeping one another alive. Friendship sustains them, but it cannot restore the youth the war has interrupted.

Paul’s sympathy also reaches beyond his own side. Guarding hungry Russian prisoners, he sees suffering men rather than the threatening enemy described from a distance. He reflects:

> A word of command has made these silent figures our enemies.

He shares cigarettes with them, although he knows they would still shoot at one another under different circumstances.

This recognition makes the fighting harder to reconcile with the certainty of the adults who encouraged it. At home, Paul encounters confident patriotic opinions but finds it difficult to explain his experience. His old interests no longer give him the same sense of belonging.

The novel follows what this does to young men who had barely begun an independent life. They have learned to endure and inflict violence, while becoming unsure what they could do afterward. Their attachment to one another remains real, even as civilian life grows harder to imagine.

## Good Will Hunting — Gus Van Sant, 1997

### A

In Gus Van Sant's *Good Will Hunting* (1997), a young janitor at MIT solves mathematics problems that challenge the university's students. Will's talent attracts a professor's attention, but it gives him no simple answer to what he wants from his life.

Will suffered abuse as a child. He can now turn an uncomfortable conversation into a contest he expects to win, using knowledge or insults to keep people from knowing him. When legal trouble brings him into therapy, he tries the same approach with Sean, his therapist.

Their conversations gradually become more personal. Will hesitates to keep dating Skylar because she seems perfect and he might discover otherwise. Sean suggests that he is afraid she will discover his own imperfections. He recalls how his late wife used to fart when she was nervous, then says:

> But those are the things I miss the most.

The embarrassing habit has become something he remembers with affection.

Will already has that kind of loyalty from his friends. Chuckie encourages him to take opportunities beyond their neighbourhood, even if it means leaving him behind. Sean and Chuckie encourage him to consider what he wants, even when pursuing it means risking disappointment.

### B

Will Hunting has loyal friends, a life in South Boston, and mathematical abilities that could take him into a very different world. In Gus Van Sant's *Good Will Hunting* (1997), the discovery of his talent brings pressure to build a career around it. Will resists being told what his intelligence obliges him to become.

His resistance also has a painful personal history. Abused as a child, he uses wit and hostility to keep new relationships under his control. With his girlfriend Skylar, affection makes him increasingly vulnerable. He invents a family history and worries about what might happen if they know each other better. Keeping his past hidden protects him from a rejection he already expects.

Sean, his therapist, responds by sharing memories of his own marriage, including the ordinary imperfections he remembers affectionately. Their conversations give Will a relationship in which he can gradually stop turning every exchange into a test of intelligence. Chuckie challenges a different defence: loyalty to their neighbourhood does not require Will to refuse every opportunity to leave.

The two men want him to have choices. Accepting their support means considering a future without being certain that he can control how other people will respond to him.
