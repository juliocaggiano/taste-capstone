## Painting: The Gross Clinic

A: medical teaching. B: creation and reception.

### A

In *The Gross Clinic* (1875), Thomas Eakins depicts surgeon Samuel D. Gross teaching during an operation. The setting is Jefferson Medical College in Philadelphia, where medical students observe surgery from tiered seats. Gross pauses to address them while five other doctors attend to the patient.

The patient has a bone infection in the left thigh. The operation removes diseased tissue from the thighbone, a procedure Gross taught for treating these infections. His assistants divide the work: one holds the incision open, while another administers an anesthetic through a folded cloth. A clerk records the proceedings.

Eakins had attended Gross's lectures while studying anatomy at Jefferson. He also observed operations and dissections, considering this knowledge essential to understanding the human body in art.

To prepare the painting, Eakins made portrait studies of Gross and several assistants and students. He also included himself among the observers.

### B

In *The Gross Clinic* (1875), Thomas Eakins depicts surgeon Samuel D. Gross teaching during an operation. The setting is Jefferson Medical College in Philadelphia, where medical students observe surgery from tiered seats. Gross pauses to address them while five other doctors attend to the patient.

Eakins prepared the painting for Philadelphia's Centennial Exhibition in 1876. At thirty-one, he hoped to establish his reputation while presenting the city's medical achievements to a wider public. He had studied anatomy at Jefferson and made portrait studies of Gross and several assistants and students.

The exhibition's art jury rejected the painting. The Philadelphia Museum of Art suggests that its explicit surgical subject may have prompted that decision. It was nevertheless displayed at the fair, in a model United States Army hospital.

A reviewer in the Philadelphia Evening Telegraph argued that it deserved a place in the art department. In 1878, Jefferson alumni bought it for $200 and gave it to the medical college.

## Book: A Room with a View

A: the narrator’s humor. B: a character’s explanation. The contextual introduction stays the same.

### A

Published in 1908, E. M. Forster's *A Room with a View* follows Lucy Honeychurch, a young Englishwoman visiting Italy. In the novel, she travels with her cousin Charlotte, who acts as her chaperone. They arrive in Florence to find that their rooms lack the promised view. Another guest, Mr Emerson, offers to exchange rooms.

Forster drew on his own travels in Italy in 1901–02. His fictional tourists stay among English guests and familiar furnishings, carrying their social expectations into the foreign setting.

Before Emerson makes his offer, each woman insists that the other should take the first available room with a view. The narrator comments:

> They were tired, and under the guise of unselfishness they wrangled.

Each tries to sound generous, but their conversation becomes an argument.

Beebe understands the offer as a practical kindness. Charlotte's concern is what accepting it might require of them.

## Film: Bicycle Thieves

A: historical and production context. B: the early pawnshop scene.

### A

*Bicycle Thieves*, directed by Vittorio De Sica in 1948, follows a fictional family in Rome after the Second World War. Antonio Ricci finds work putting up posters, but he needs a bicycle for the job. When it is stolen on his first day, he searches the city with his young son, Bruno.

Italy faced widespread unemployment when the film was made. De Sica filmed on Rome's streets and used nonprofessional actors, two practices associated with Italian neorealism. This approach brought contemporary surroundings and unfamiliar faces into a story about work and family.

Research helped shape that choice of setting. Screenwriter Suso Cecchi D'Amico later described how she and De Sica sought people and places rarely represented in cinema. Casting also drew on people outside the profession. Enzo Staiola, who plays Bruno, recalled being discovered by De Sica while walking home from school. A professional crew staged the street scenes, including large crowds and an artificial rainstorm.

### B

*Bicycle Thieves*, directed by Vittorio De Sica in 1948, follows a fictional family in Rome after the Second World War. Antonio Ricci finds work putting up posters, but he needs a bicycle for the job. When it is stolen on his first day, he searches the city with his young son, Bruno.

Before Antonio can start work, his wife Maria takes their bedsheets to a pawnshop. Their bicycle has already been pawned; the money from the linen lets them reclaim it. Maria's decision makes the new job possible. The family gives up the use of one possession to recover another that can help them earn a wage.

At the pawnshop, the camera reveals a large store of similar linen. The shelves suggest other households have made comparable transactions, extending the scene beyond Antonio and Maria's immediate problem.
