## The Raft of the Medusa — Théodore Géricault, 1818–1819

### A

In July 1816, the French frigate Méduse ran aground off West Africa while carrying officials and settlers to Senegal. Its captain had obtained his command through royal connections despite having spent twenty years away from the sea.

When the ship could not be freed, the captain and governor left in boats. About 150 people were placed on a makeshift raft, which was cut loose from the boats towing it. Only fifteen remained alive when help arrived almost two weeks later. The survivors’ accounts made the wreck a scandal for France’s recently restored monarchy.

Géricault’s painting shows the stranded men trying to attract a distant ship. Dead and exhausted figures occupy the foreground; above them, men gather around a Black survivor waving a cloth. Their movements direct attention toward a possible rescue, while the seated figure holding a body does not join the signalling.

Exhibited in 1819, the painting gave this recent failure of authority the enormous scale associated with prestigious history painting.

### B

When Géricault exhibited The Raft of the Medusa in 1819, the shipwreck was already a political scandal. The captain’s royal connections had secured him a command for which he was poorly prepared. His ship ran aground on its way to Senegal, and about 150 people were eventually abandoned on a raft. The captain and governor escaped in boats; only fifteen people on the raft remained alive when help arrived.

Géricault met survivors while preparing the painting. He shows the uncertainty before rescue, with a ship barely visible on the horizon. Some men still make an effort to signal it. Others lie dead or exhausted, and one seated figure holds a body without joining the movement around him.

At more than seven metres across, the painting gives these abandoned passengers the scale usually associated with military victories and ancient heroes.

## Nineteen Eighty-Four — George Orwell, 1949

### A

George Orwell’s *Nineteen Eighty-Four*, published in 1949, follows Winston Smith in a fictional London ruled by the Party. His job is to rewrite old newspaper reports so that they agree with the Party’s current version of events.

This gives the state more than a convincing public story. If the original records disappear, people lose the evidence needed to dispute it. Winston’s uncertain memories leave him afraid that he may be the only person who remembers differently.

The Party also develops Newspeak, a language intended to make disagreement impossible even in thought. Syme, who works on its dictionary, tells Winston:

> It's a beautiful thing, the destruction of words.

The two projects reinforce each other: one removes evidence of a different past, the other aims to remove ways of questioning the present. In his 1946 essay *The Prevention of Literature*, Orwell had argued that rulers claiming to be infallible must continually alter history. In the novel, Winston’s secret diary attempts to preserve a version of experience outside that official account.

### B

George Orwell’s *Nineteen Eighty-Four*, published in 1949, imagines a state that demands control over people’s beliefs and feelings. Its protagonist, Winston Smith, secretly doubts the Party while helping it falsify historical records. He wants to know whether his memories are reliable and whether anyone else shares his doubts.

Surveillance makes finding out dangerous. Winston must watch his expression, his gestures, and what he says. The narration describes the small space he still considers private:

> Nothing was your own except the few cubic centimetres inside your skull.

That sense of isolation also shapes his relationships. Children are encouraged to report their parents, and neighbours can become informers. Affection offers no assurance of safety. The Party’s demand for loyalty extends into the home, where ordinary remarks can become evidence against someone.

Winston begins a diary and later a secret relationship with Julia. These give his resistance a personal purpose: preserving his own account of events and sharing a life that the Party does not arrange.

## Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind — Michel Gondry, 2004

### A

In Michel Gondry's *Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind* (2004), Joel discovers that his former girlfriend has erased him from her memory. Hurt by Clementine's decision, he arranges to undergo the same procedure. The film invents a medical solution to a familiar wish after a breakup: to stop being reminded of someone.

The treatment works backwards through their relationship. Recent arguments give way to earlier moments of affection, and Joel begins to resist. In one memory, Clementine describes feeling ugly as a child. She had a doll she hoped to transform, imagining that she might become beautiful too. Joel reassures her, then asks the doctor:

> Please let me keep this memory.

Joel’s change of mind comes from revisiting a relationship he has begun to judge entirely through its painful ending. Their earlier affection matters to him again, even though it cannot settle their disagreements. The treatment would erase the quarrels together with the moments when they understood and comforted each other.

### B

After a breakup, photographs and gifts can become unwanted reminders of a former partner. *Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind* (2004) takes that familiar impulse further. Joel gathers objects connected to Clementine for a clinic that promises to erase his memories of her. She has already undergone the procedure, and he wants relief from the hurt of remembering someone who no longer remembers him.

Michel Gondry's film follows the treatment inside Joel's mind, moving from recent unhappiness towards earlier intimacy. As the memories disappear, his anger gives way to a wish to protect them. One recalls Clementine confessing her childhood fear of being ugly. Joel's reassurance recalls something the breakup has obscured: they once trusted each other with feelings they found difficult to admit.

The imagined treatment treats attachment as something that can be removed. Joel's resistance complicates that promise. He wants to stop suffering, but he also wants to keep the experience of having known Clementine closely. Following the memories backwards makes those wishes increasingly difficult to separate.
