## Vidas Secas

### A

Published in 1938, Graciliano Ramos’s *Vidas Secas* follows a fictional family of *retirantes*: people driven from their homes by drought and poverty. Fabiano, Sinha Vitória, their two boys and their dog, Baleia, cross Brazil’s northeastern interior looking for food and work. Finding a farm where they can stay offers relief, but little security.

Their situation belongs to a rural economy in which landowners controlled both employment and access to land. Brazil’s political changes after 1930 left that ownership structure intact. In the novel, Fabiano tends another man’s cattle, accepts dishonest accounts and retreats when threatened with dismissal. Rain can restore the pasture without changing who controls his livelihood. [FGV](https://atlas.fgv.br/verbete/5863), [João Roberto Maia](https://www.scielo.br/j/alea/a/rNBYPktCKPyMkZTp875f7Gj/)

Ramos had himself been imprisoned without formal charges under Getúlio Vargas in 1936. He later credited living alongside poor prisoners with helping him write about their lives. His narration gives Fabiano thoughts he cannot easily express to an employer or policeman. “You’re an animal, Fabiano,” he tells himself, accepting his low position while also taking pride in his endurance. Sinha Vitória’s calculations, the boys’ curiosity and Baleia’s affection give each a life beyond the work of surviving. [Casa Museu](https://www.casagracilianoramos.com.br/sala-6-memorias-do-carcere/1936-), [Zenir Campos Reis](https://www.scielo.br/j/ea/a/4P76p7GKXFft33XjbtKdLqk/?format=html&lang=pt)

The repeated departures connect their story to Brazil’s history of internal migration. The parents imagine a city where the boys could attend school. The narrator anticipates them becoming trapped there. Elsewhere in Brazil, this movement was already being organised as a source of labor. From 1935, São Paulo subsidised workers’ journeys to its coffee and cotton farms. Leaving the drought could mean entering another economy that needed their labor without guaranteeing them a better life. [Museu da Imigração](https://www.museudaimigracao.org.br/en/blog/conhecendo-o-acervo/brasileiros-na-hospedaria-percursos-ocupacao-e-trabalho)

### B

Graciliano Ramos wrote *Vidas Secas* after his release from prison in 1937. Arrested the previous year under Getúlio Vargas, he had spent months confined without formal charges. He later explained that living with poor prisoners had helped him understand people he had hesitated to portray. Published in 1938, the novel follows a fictional family whose encounters with authority repeatedly expose how little protection they have. [Casa Museu](https://www.casagracilianoramos.com.br/sala-6-memorias-do-carcere/1936-), [Zenir Campos Reis](https://www.scielo.br/j/ea/a/4P76p7GKXFft33XjbtKdLqk/?format=html&lang=pt)

Fabiano and Sinha Vitória travel through the sertão, Brazil’s northeastern interior, with their two boys and their dog, Baleia. They are *retirantes*, displaced by drought and poverty. Fabiano finds work tending cattle, but owns neither the herd nor the land. His employer can underpay him and threaten dismissal; a policeman can provoke him, then have him beaten and jailed. These relationships keep the family vulnerable even when the rains return. [João Roberto Maia](https://www.scielo.br/j/alea/a/rNBYPktCKPyMkZTp875f7Gj/)

Ramos follows what each family member thinks, often without being able to say it. Sinha Vitória wants a proper bed. The boys want explanations for the world around them. Baleia has memories, fears and attachments of her own. This attention makes the family’s poverty an experience that shapes what they can hope for, learn and share with one another. [Ramos’s 1938 interview](https://graciliano.com.br/2014/11/nov-14-conversa-sobre-vidas-secas/)

Their hope of finding a different life belongs to a larger historical movement. In the 1930s, São Paulo began subsidising domestic migrants to supply its farms with workers. The parents imagine a new life in the city. The narrator warns that they would become trapped there, with more families following the same route: “And the backlands would keep sending people there.” Their journey is one among many, sustained by conditions that keep forcing other families to leave. [Museu da Imigração](https://www.museudaimigracao.org.br/en/blog/conhecendo-o-acervo/brasileiros-na-hospedaria-percursos-ocupacao-e-trabalho), [Vidas Secas](https://brasiliana.usp.br/handle/bbm/8399)

## I’m Still Here

### A

In 1964, Brazil’s military coup removed an elected government and stripped congressman Rubens Paiva of his mandate. In January 1971, agents took him from his Rio de Janeiro home. He was tortured and killed in custody; the authorities concealed the crime and circulated a false account of his escape. These events underpin Walter Salles’s *I’m Still Here* (2024), adapted from Marcelo Rubens Paiva’s memoir about his family. [Câmara dos Deputados](https://www.camara.leg.br/deputados/131119/biografia)

The film follows Rubens’s wife, Eunice, who is also imprisoned, along with their teenage daughter Eliana. After her release, she must raise five children while seeking answers from the authorities responsible. When she confronts the men watching her home, she asks: “What are you looking at? What do you want? Where’s my husband?” The government’s concealment makes grief harder: the family cannot bury Rubens or obtain an official account of his death. [CNN](https://transcripts.cnn.com/show/ampr/date/2025-02-14/segment/01)

Salles spends time with the family before the arrest, showing their music, visitors and easy conversation. Those scenes establish the freedom that disappears from their home. Afterwards, Eunice tries to keep the children’s lives going while deciding how much of her fear and knowledge to share. The dictatorship’s effects emerge through these ordinary responsibilities. [Salles and Torres](https://letterboxd.com/journal/im-still-here-fernanda-torres-walter-salles-interview/)

Historically, Eunice’s response extended into public life. She entered law school in 1973 and became a lawyer defending Indigenous rights. Her campaign helped secure official recognition of the dictatorship’s disappeared. Rubens’s death certificate was finally issued in 1996, twenty-five years after his arrest. That delay explains why acknowledgment matters so much in the film: the family has had to fight for the state to recognise a death its own agents caused. [Instituto Vladimir Herzog](https://memoriasdaditadura.org.br/personagens/eunice-paiva/)

### B

Walter Salles’s *I’m Still Here* (2024) tells the Paiva family’s story under Brazil’s military dictatorship. Rubens Paiva had lost his congressional mandate after the 1964 coup. In 1971, the regime arrested, tortured and killed him, then concealed its responsibility. The film stays with his wife, Eunice, and their five children, who must continue living while being denied the truth about his fate. [Câmara dos Deputados](https://www.camara.leg.br/deputados/131119/biografia)

This is the history behind Eunice’s question, “Where’s my husband?” She is addressing men connected to the state that took him. Eunice and her teenage daughter are themselves detained; afterwards, even the family home remains under surveillance. Her effort to protect the children requires continuing their daily routines while challenging officials who can still harm them. [CNN](https://transcripts.cnn.com/show/ampr/date/2025-02-14/segment/01)

When journalists photograph the family, Eunice insists that they smile. She wants Rubens’s case made public, but refuses a picture that shows them only as defeated. Earlier home movies and photographs have already established him as a father and husband. Preserving those memories gives the children a record of the person the authorities have removed from their lives. [Salles and Torres](https://letterboxd.com/journal/im-still-here-fernanda-torres-walter-salles-interview/)

Eunice’s real life continued through law school, work defending Indigenous rights and a campaign for the recognition of the dictatorship’s disappeared. She received Rubens’s death certificate only in 1996. [Instituto Vladimir Herzog](https://memoriasdaditadura.org.br/personagens/eunice-paiva/)

Eunice later developed Alzheimer’s disease. Marcelo wrote his memoir as she was losing her memory, preserving the family’s history. Salles’s adaptation extends that work of remembrance, making an experience once concealed by the government part of Brazil’s public memory. [Salles and Torres](https://transcripts.cnn.com/show/ampr/date/2025-02-14/segment/01)
