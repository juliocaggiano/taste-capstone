## Research basis

1. **Exact caption and image:** photographer portfolio, above. Treat its caption’s speculative slave-raider origin as unverified and exclude it. Do not import its stereotypes from adjacent captions.
2. **Production and purpose:** [Melissa Leong’s 2010 interview](https://melissawleong.wordpress.com/2010/11/15/from-the-jonas-brothers-to-ethiopian-tribesmen/), read in full. It supports the deliberate pose, lighting and contemporary presentation. It does not prove the sitter’s consent terms or payment.
3. **Cultural explanation:** [Mursi Online, Lip-plates](https://www.mursi.org/introducing-the-mursi/Body%20Decoration/lip-plates), read in full. The entry takes a concise account of form, adult identity and variation. It avoids treating the page’s broad claim about voluntary practice as a complete account.
4. **Market context:** [Mursi Online, Body Decoration](https://www.mursi.org/introducing-the-mursi/Body%20Decoration), read in full. The short paragraph explains income and visitor-oriented decoration. It does not assert that Engalo’s exact adornment was invented for this sitting or that she was paid.
5. **Community voices and challenge:** [Shauna LaTosky’s fieldwork chapter](https://www.mursi.org/documents-and-texts/published-articles/shauna-latosky/the-lip-plates-of-mursi-women), especially printed pages 385–392. It supplies disagreements and social pressure missing from a simple beauty explanation. The women quoted in this research are not Engalo. The draft explicitly says so.

## Meaning and omission check

The story connects a named portrait to cultural belonging, social expectations and tourist exchange. Its final recorded voice challenges an assumed choice between appearance and education. The reader learns what the plate can mean without receiving a fictional biography of Engalo.

The draft does not claim all women choose freely. It acknowledges pain and family pressure. Nor does it equate coercion, tourist expectations and personal pride. The broader study includes threats of violence; this entry’s wording signals constraint without presenting those reported threats as Engalo’s experience.

The photograph itself does not disclose these debates. Its framing remains the point of entry; the ethnographic account is visibly introduced as external research. The ending is a participant’s specific statement, not an assistant-written dramatic conclusion.
