## Editorial approach

Read VOICE_GUIDE v0.21 and the accumulated corrections before drafting. Each entry has a meaning brief, a readable account supported by specific details, and separate source records. A second pass checked that the central interpretation actually appears in the prose. A third pass checked unfamiliar names, overstatement, chronology, endings and image identity. ## Meaning and evidence audit

| Work | Central account in the prose | Essential detail and context | Misreading avoided |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Milkmaid | Sustained attention to a servant’s work makes an ordinary task worthy of serious painting. | Concentrated pouring, tactile surfaces, removed background objects; Dutch domestic ideals and maid stereotypes. | A definite romantic thought, an established political manifesto, or a scene described only as peaceful. |
| The Night Watch | A civic commission represents collective identity and command through action. | Armed citizen company, captain and lieutenant, musketeer actions, emblematic girl. | A nocturnal battle, a random child, or the unsupported story that angry sitters ended Rembrandt’s career. |
| Judith Beheading Holofernes | Two women cooperate to save a threatened community. | Bethulia explained before the killing; maid’s active restraint and Judith’s physical effort. | Confusing the Naples and Florence versions; asserting that the painting is literally revenge for rape. |
| The Calling of Saint Matthew | A religious invitation reaches a distrusted person during ordinary work. | Tax collection, money, Christ’s gesture, light, contemporary clothes. | Omitting grace/conversion or treating the identification of the bearded figure as beyond dispute. |
| The Surrender of Breda | A defeated enemy’s dignity becomes part of a victor’s political self-image. | The key exchange, shoulder gesture and royal Hall of Realms commission. | Presenting courtesy without the war and propaganda context; repeating absolute claims that conquest caused no harm. |
| American Gothic | Rural stability and respectability can also look restrictive. | Father and daughter, Gothic house, Depression and divided reception. | Calling them husband and wife, or calling the work unambiguously satire against Wood’s stated positive intention. |
| Abaporu | Brazilian artists transform external influences instead of accepting European cultural authority. | Birthday gift, manifesto, cultural cannibalism explained, Paris training. | Claiming that a small head conclusively means ignorance; treating the image as Indigenous ethnographic documentation. |
| The Broken Column | A patient makes otherwise invisible pain intelligible while retaining her own presence. | Accident context, orthopedic brace, column/spine, nails, composed face with tears. | Claiming recovery or reducing the artist’s entire career to misery; diagnosing her medically. |
| La Grande Jatte | Public leisure retains social distinctions and conventions. | Dress, rigid spacing, ancient sculptural order and separate color marks. | Describing only the dot technique; asserting a single documented political intention or a definite occupation for each woman. |
| The Birth of Venus | Renaissance interest in antiquity gives a classical image of love and beauty a new setting. | Arrival rather than literal birth, ancient pose, Florentine domestic culture. | A proved anti-Christian rebellion, a certain Medici commission, or a single uncontested Neoplatonic program. |

## Sources and access limits

Every entry’s `sources` list records its supporting URLs and precise role. Museum collection records and curatorial accounts anchor the interpretation. Public discussions supplied challenges and reception clues; they do not establish facts or consensus.

- **Milkmaid:** the current Rijksmuseum object record gives **c. 1660**, which takes precedence over older 1657–58 wording in the technical press release. The Met essay supplied the domestic and sexual-stereotype context. The 2022 Rijksmuseum investigation supplied the removed objects. Cupid is presented as a possible allusion, not a statement of the servant’s thoughts.
- **Night Watch:** read both Rijksmuseum articles. The Khan/Smarthistory account was available as indexed text, but its complete page body was not accessible. The company-emblem interpretation is also recorded in the image’s established catalog context. No claims depend on patrons rejecting the painting. Modern scan margins are part of the extant canvas, not generated reconstructions of the pieces removed in 1715.
- **Judith:** Uffizi identifies its canvas as **c. 1620**. A National Gallery exhibition notice gives an earlier dating for the Florence version. Prefer the holding museum’s current catalog date and do not conflate the two paintings. The National Gallery’s heroine page discusses another moment in the Judith story; it supports the story and agency, not the exact visual description here. Rape biography is qualified instead of becoming the sole explanation.
- **Matthew:** read Smarthistory’s account and the available official Rome guide. The identification debate matters, so the body says “usually identified.” The reader does not need an additional paragraph of competing names to understand the religious meaning.
- **Breda:** the Prado’s accessible explanation supplies the key political interpretation. Its main collection page was unavailable. WGA supports the 1634–35 date and royal decorative program. Avoid its absolute statement that citizens suffered no harm.
- **American Gothic:** the Art Institute’s regular webpage returned an access error. Its public collection API returned the full description and catalog metadata; saved as `american-api.html` in QA. Read Whitney’s curator transcript and exhibition page for the affirmative/satirical reception distinction. Formal discussion of repeated verticals is visual analysis, not an assertion of private intent.
- **Abaporu:** the MALBA page was accessible through a public HTTP download, although the web reader failed. Its full essay by Regina Teixeira de Barros was read. MoMA supplied the curator/artist transcript, and UNESP preserved MASP’s curatorial text. Conflicting public explanations of the figure’s proportions were a reason to avoid speculative codes. The cultural movement is not presented as a faithful statement of Indigenous beliefs.
- **Broken Column:** Casa de México publishes a Museo Dolores Olmedo text by Josefina García. The Frida Kahlo museum timeline supports the 1925 accident. Public discussion included readers describing chronic-illness recognition and objections to reducing her life to suffering. These reactions informed emphasis without being quoted or generalized.
- **La Grande Jatte:** the Art Institute’s archived exhibition essay was fully accessible. Smarthistory’s original page body was not retrievable, but indexed passages establish the ancient-frieze comparison; its Neo-Impressionism article provides indexed support for social tension. Public discussion contained both social-critique and shared-leisure readings, plus objections to unattributed copying in the thread. No Reddit wording was copied. The later painted border is retained in images; the main creation date stays 1884–1886.
- **Venus:** read the Uffizi record and Wikipedia’s stylistic/interpretive history alongside the saved Notion study. Uffizi’s **c. 1485** supplies the date. The precise commission and Neoplatonic scheme are not treated as established facts.

## Image review

Thirty separately sourced image files are available locally. No AI generation, local recoloring or local artwork crop was used. Thumbnails use the existing review layout and each opens the full downloaded image.

Identity checks confirmed the Uffizi Judith, the original Abaporu rather than Antropofagia, and final La Grande Jatte rather than a preparatory study. Contact sheets and source metadata are in `qa/editorial-batch-005-2026-09-26/`.

- Rejected a Night Watch photograph with broad blue glare and an Abaporu photograph with reflected gallery lights. Both candidate files remain as unused research assets; the review uses cleaner replacements.
- Judith B and La Grande Jatte C deliberately retain a museum frame as part of their separate source photograph. Their labels disclose this. The painting itself remains complete.
- Breda B is a source-provided adjusted reproduction, downloaded without further changes. Its treatment is disclosed in the image details.
- Some reproductions differ markedly in color. They are alternatives for editorial choice, not assertions that every file is color accurate. The holding institution’s reproduction is preferred as a reference where available.
- The Broken Column B is a smaller 400 × 535 source and is labeled accordingly. Its stronger color rendition offers a real comparison; it has not been upscaled or sharpened to manufacture detail.
- Sources and copyright notices are retained. Public review access does not itself clear an image for public app distribution. Approval remains separate from licensing and import.
