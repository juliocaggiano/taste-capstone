## Analysis note: The Picture of Dorian Gray

- **Central concern:** the wish to preserve youth separates Dorian’s admired appearance from his worsening moral life. The account must explain both the wish and his subsequent choices.
- **Human experience:** Henry intensifies fear of aging and losing admiration. Dorian experiences initial concern, the desire for pleasure, self-protection, and fascination with the contrast between his image and conduct. Do not reduce him to permanent guilt or an automatic magical transformation into evil.
- **Development and evidence:** chapter II wish; chapter VII cruelty registered in the mouth and initial resolution to use the portrait as conscience; later concealment and repeated comparison with his reflection. The quotations mark desire and initial recognition.
- **Social/cultural connection:** the fashionable London society represented in the novel can continue to value beauty and social appearance despite reports of misconduct. This supports the story’s moral conflict; it does not substitute for explaining it. The entry does not make a universal empirical claim about Victorian people.
- **Evidence:** [book research](research-2026-09-20/ROUND_10_BOOK_SOURCES.md), [root primary-text extracts](research-2026-09-20/ROUND_10_BOOK_PRIMARY_EXTRACTS.txt), original English novel and British Library account. These are proposed repairs; Julio did not list the missing points himself.

## Revised book entry

In Oscar Wilde’s *The Picture of Dorian Gray*, the young Dorian becomes frightened of losing the beauty that brings him admiration. Lord Henry has encouraged him to regard youth and pleasure as the most important things in life. Looking at Basil Hallward’s portrait of him, Dorian wishes that it would age while he remained young:

> I would give my soul for that!

His wish comes true, but the painting also begins to show the cruelty of his behaviour. After he mistreats a young actress, he notices a cruel expression around its mouth. His own face remains unchanged. The portrait makes his moral deterioration visible while his beauty conceals it from others.

Dorian initially recognizes what the change means and resolves to behave differently. The narration describes the role he hopes the painting will play:

> The picture, changed or unchanged, would be to him the visible emblem of conscience.

Later, he hides it in a locked room and continues pursuing pleasure at other people’s expense. His youthful appearance helps him remain welcome in fashionable London society even as rumours about him spread. The novel follows his growing willingness to protect that appearance while avoiding responsibility for his actions. He repeatedly returns to the portrait, sometimes distressed, sometimes fascinated by the difference between its damaged face and his own.

## Analysis note: Parasite

- **Central concern:** wealth and signs of status shape the recognition people receive. The Kims maintain invented identities to enter an admired social world; class contempt persists when their origins become perceptible.
- **Human experience:** desire for respect and security; fear of losing a new position; shame and humiliation when acceptable conduct fails to secure recognition. These are supported interpretations of actions and reactions.
- **Development and evidence:** the family’s false identities and displacement of staff; the Parks’ cultivated sophistication; Mr Park’s remark about smell and Ki-taek’s reaction. The quoted exchange directly demonstrates class judgment.
- **Social/cultural connection:** the critique concerns the class hierarchy represented in a contemporary Seoul setting. It shows status affecting personal judgment and how appearances are maintained. Do not state that all South Koreans judge others only by money. Do not equate the family’s deception with innate virtue or universal necessity.
- **Evidence:** [film research](research-2026-09-20/ROUND_10_FILM_SOURCES.md): Bong’s GQ interview, Criterion’s account, and a finished-film scholarly transcription. This supplies both interpretation and specific supporting scenes.

## Revised film entry

Bong Joon Ho’s *Parasite* (2019) uses two families in Seoul to examine how wealth becomes a measure of a person’s social worth. The poor Kim family gains access to the wealthy Parks’ household by presenting themselves as unrelated, well-qualified professionals. Their invented backgrounds help secure a respect they do not receive as themselves.

They conceal their family ties, adopt prestigious identities, and arrange the dismissal of other workers so their relatives can replace them. The film connects their desire for a better life with the pressure to hide where they come from.

The Parks also cultivate an image of sophistication through their house, taste, and manners. Yet they expect the people serving them to remain socially separate. Mr Park’s private conversation with his wife makes this distinction clear. His driver, Ki-taek, behaves as expected, but Mr Park objects to his smell:

> But, that smell crosses the line.

Ki-taek hears the remark and smells his own shirt. His employer’s disgust makes his poverty feel like a personal defect.
