## Painting: The Ambassadors

A explains the skull through portraiture, mortality, and Christian context. B explains how changing viewpoint produces that reading. Both make the skull central.

### A

Hans Holbein the Younger’s *The Ambassadors* (1533) presents two French diplomats surrounded by signs of wealth and learning. Across the foreground, however, a skull appears stretched almost beyond recognition. It comes into proportion when viewed obliquely from the right.

This distortion, called anamorphosis, is central to how the painting works. From the front, the men’s clothes, instruments, and books establish their standing. A change of position brings mortality into view. The skull belongs to the tradition of *memento mori*: images that remind viewers of death, including within portraits made to preserve someone’s achievements.

The painting therefore asks its viewers to consider the men’s accomplishments alongside the limits of their lives. Its religious context also matters. A small crucifix is partly hidden behind the curtain at the upper left. Read together, the skull and crucifix connect death with the Christian hope of salvation.

### B

In Hans Holbein the Younger’s *The Ambassadors* (1533), two French diplomats stand among books, globes, and scientific instruments. Their possessions suggest education and influence. Yet the painting’s most disruptive object is the skull stretched across the floor between them.

Holbein makes it difficult to examine the men and the skull in normal proportion from the same position. Seen from the front, the portrait is clear and the skull distorted. Viewed from the right, the skull resolves while the rest of the painting becomes oblique. This technique is called anamorphosis.

The change of viewpoint gives the reminder of death a particular role. The men’s status dominates the first view; their mortality becomes clear from another. The painting can be read as a reflection on the limits of wealth and knowledge, expressed through the act of looking. At the upper left, a partly hidden crucifix connects this reminder of death with the Christian hope of salvation.

## Book: The Metamorphosis

A examines internalized duty and the loss of the provider role. B examines continuing inner life and diminished human recognition. Both address alienation and preserve initial care.

### A

Franz Kafka’s *The Metamorphosis* (1915) begins with Gregor Samsa waking in an insect-like body. He has supported his parents and sister through a job he dislikes. His transformation leaves him unable to provide for them, and the story follows his increasing exclusion from family life.

One of Gregor’s first concerns is getting to work:

> I’ve got to get up, my train leaves at five.

*Gregor’s thoughts, translated by David Wyllie.*

That response shows how strongly work governs his understanding of himself. Even a change he cannot explain becomes a problem of lateness and disappointing others. His employer sends the chief clerk to demand an explanation that morning.

At home, his sister Grete initially brings him food and cleans his room. Her care gradually gives way to impatience and rejection. Gregor still feels responsibility and affection, while others increasingly regard him as a burden. Kafka examines what happens to someone’s place in a family when he can no longer fulfil his role as provider.

### B

Franz Kafka’s *The Metamorphosis* (1915) follows Gregor Samsa, a travelling salesman who wakes in an insect-like body. His family can no longer understand his speech, but the reader continues to follow his thoughts. That difference is central: the person whose feelings we know becomes increasingly difficult for those around him to recognize as a person.

His sister Grete initially tries to meet his changed needs. Gregor also continues to care about her. Hearing her play the violin, he is drawn toward the music. The narration expresses his response through a question:

> Was he an animal if music could captivate him so?

*The narration follows Gregor’s thoughts; translated by David Wyllie.*

His response to music shows an aspect of him that cannot be reduced to earning a living. As the family increasingly treats him as an unwanted creature, the reader still encounters his feelings and desires. His altered appearance becomes a reason to disregard feelings the reader knows he still has.

## Film: The Truman Show

A examines how Truman can recognize a constructed reality. B examines how trusted surroundings and fear restrict his apparent choices. Both address control and agency.

### A

Peter Weir’s *The Truman Show* (1998) follows a man whose ordinary life has been arranged for television. Truman Burbank does not know that his town is a set, or that his wife and friends are actors. He trusts them because they belong to the only world he has known.

The film’s central concern is how someone can recognize and question a life controlled by others. The production supplies both Truman’s experiences and the people who help him interpret them. Its influence reaches into what he considers normal, whom he believes, and which possibilities seem available to him.

An early incident makes this control briefly visible: his car radio picks up instructions from the production team. What seemed to be an ordinary journey has been coordinated around him. The significance of such moments is that they give Truman reasons to doubt familiar explanations. Making choices of his own first requires him to question the account of his life that the production has taught him to accept.

### B

Peter Weir’s *The Truman Show* (1998) follows Truman Burbank, who unknowingly lives inside a television production. His town is a set, and the people closest to him play assigned roles. The apparent familiarity of his life helps keep him from recognizing how extensively it is controlled.

The programme discourages him from leaving through messages that seem to come from ordinary sources. A travel agency displays images of crashing planes. Television praises the benefits of staying home. These details show how the production shapes his decisions by making the outside world seem dangerous and his familiar surroundings reassuring.

Truman experiences those fears and attachments as his own. He has no reason, at first, to connect them with a producer’s need to keep him in the show. The film explores how a person’s choices can be restricted through the world he has learned to trust. His growing desire to see beyond Seahaven puts that trust into question, along with the life built around it.
