## Painting: The Ambassadors

A develops diplomacy and personalized geography. B explains scientific objects and the use of distorted perspective.

### A

Hans Holbein the Younger painted *The Ambassadors* in 1533, while the French ambassador Jean de Dinteville was in London. His friend Georges de Selve, Bishop of Lavaur, stands beside him. The two men pose with globes, books, musical instruments, and equipment for astronomical observations.

Dinteville represented Francis I of France during Henry VIII’s break with Rome. That year, Henry married Anne Boleyn despite the pope’s refusal to annul his first marriage. Dinteville’s work included reporting on these developments to the French king.

Objects in Renaissance portraits often helped describe a sitter’s interests and accomplishments. Here, even the geography has a personal connection. The globe on the lower shelf includes Polisy, the small settlement where Dinteville had his château. Holbein places it northeast of Paris, although it actually lies southeast. The National Gallery’s catalogue explains that this change made the place and its label more prominent. The painting itself later hung in the château at Polisy.

### B

Hans Holbein the Younger painted *The Ambassadors* in 1533, while the French ambassador Jean de Dinteville was in London. His friend Georges de Selve, Bishop of Lavaur, stands beside him. The two men pose with globes, books, musical instruments, and equipment for astronomical observations.

The upper shelf holds instruments for measuring time and the positions of celestial bodies. Such objects connected mathematics with court life. Nicholas Kratzer, Henry VIII’s astronomer and an instrument maker, also collaborated with Holbein on designs for court festivities.

Their painted precision can be misleading. On the many-sided sundial, the arrangement of hour lines would prevent the instrument from working correctly. The National Gallery’s technical catalogue identifies several similar inconsistencies in the instruments.

A deliberate distortion appears at the men’s feet. Seen straight on, the pale shape looks stretched and difficult to identify. From the right, it resolves into a skull. This use of perspective is called anamorphosis: the image becomes recognizable from a particular viewing position.

## Book: The Metamorphosis

A develops workplace authority and time. B develops paid work and family obligations. Both use the same brief passage.

### A

Published in German in 1915, Franz Kafka’s *The Metamorphosis* follows Gregor Samsa, a travelling salesman who wakes with an insect-like body. He cannot work or make himself understood, and his family must adjust to losing its provider.

Gregor still thinks about catching his train:

> I’ve got to get up, my train leaves at five.

*Gregor’s thoughts, translated by David Wyllie.*

His employer’s chief clerk comes to the apartment that morning, demanding an explanation. Gregor has hardly begun to understand his condition before he must defend his reliability as an employee.

Kafka had experience of demanding office schedules. At his first insurance job, in Prague, the working day ran from eight until six, with a two-hour break. The firm could require unpaid overtime; employees could apply for fourteen days’ leave only once every two years. Kafka left in 1908 for the Workers’ Accident Insurance Institute, where he remained for fourteen years.

### B

Published in German in 1915, Franz Kafka’s *The Metamorphosis* follows Gregor Samsa, a travelling salesman who wakes with an insect-like body. He cannot work or make himself understood, and his family must adjust to losing its provider.

His first morning still revolves around work:

> I’ve got to get up, my train leaves at five.

*Gregor’s thoughts, translated by David Wyllie.*

Gregor dislikes his job, but his parents owe money to his employer. Resigning would affect the whole household. After the transformation, his sister Grete takes responsibility for feeding him and cleaning his room. Earning money and providing care become separate problems for the family.

Kafka’s own obligations also included a family business. In 1911, his brother-in-law Karl Hermann recruited him to help manage a new asbestos factory. Kafka already had a job in workers’ insurance, so the business added another set of duties. In a letter to Max Brod in October 1912, he complained that supervising its workers would interrupt his writing.

## Film: The Truman Show

A develops advertising within ordinary life. B develops privacy and permission.

### A

Peter Weir's *The Truman Show* (1998) follows Truman Burbank, an insurance salesman who unknowingly stars in a television programme. The fictional town where he lives is a studio set, and the people closest to him are actors. Viewers watch his daily life while he believes it is private.

When his wife Meryl presents a kitchen gadget, she describes its features with the enthusiasm of a commercial. For Truman, this is a conversation at home. For the programme, it is also an opportunity to sell something. This is product placement: advertising incorporated into the action.

The programme relies on Truman responding naturally to situations its producers arrange. His reactions can be genuine while the people around him perform for the audience and promote products. His unawareness helps make those commercial encounters look like ordinary life.

### B

Peter Weir's *The Truman Show* (1998) follows Truman Burbank, an insurance salesman who unknowingly stars in a television programme. The fictional town where he lives is a studio set, and the people closest to him are actors. Viewers watch his daily life while he believes it is private.

In the opening, Truman talks to himself in the bathroom mirror. A concealed camera makes this private routine available to strangers. Weir recalled developing the mirror idea with Jim Carrey during rehearsal, turning something a person might do alone into material for an audience.

The scene separates visibility from permission. Viewers know things about Truman that he has never chosen to share. The producers also arrange his surroundings, which influence the decisions he makes. His trust in familiar people gives the programme access that an openly identified camera crew would have to negotiate. The film connects privacy with control over participation: Truman cannot decide what to disclose, or whether to participate at all, because he does not know he is being filmed.
