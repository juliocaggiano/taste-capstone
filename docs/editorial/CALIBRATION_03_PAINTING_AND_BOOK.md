## Painting: The Thankful Poor

Henry Ossawa Tanner, 1894. Oil on canvas. Art Bridges collection.

### A: Historical overview

Painted in 1894, *The Thankful Poor* shows an older Black man and a boy saying grace before a meal. Henry Ossawa Tanner had begun studying in Paris in 1891. During a return visit to the United States in 1893–1894, he concentrated on scenes of African American daily life. [P1][P3][P4]

Tanner wanted to broaden the way Black people appeared in American painting. In an account of his work, he criticized artists who treated Black life mainly as a source of comedy. His own subjects included religious observance and the teaching of music, activities he presented through everyday encounters. [P3]

The religious setting also had a connection to his upbringing. His father, Benjamin Tucker Tanner, was a bishop in the African Methodist Episcopal Church. Faith would remain a recurring subject in his work, though he soon changed the kinds of scenes he painted. [P1][P2]

After returning to France, Tanner increasingly concentrated on biblical subjects. His paintings of Daniel in the lions' den and the raising of Lazarus brought him recognition at the Paris Salon in 1896 and 1897. *The Thankful Poor* is his last known scene of African American daily life. [P1][P4]

### B: Social practice and interpretation

Painted in 1894, *The Thankful Poor* shows an older Black man and a boy saying grace before a meal. Henry Ossawa Tanner had begun studying in Paris in 1891. During a return visit to the United States in 1893–1894, he concentrated on scenes of African American daily life. [P1][P3][P4]

The subject reflected Tanner's interest in presenting Black life beyond the comic stereotypes he criticized in other artists' work. The previous year, he had painted *The Banjo Lesson*, in which an older man teaches a boy to play. Both works concentrate on an activity shared by an adult and a child. [P3]

Tanner's father was a bishop in the African Methodist Episcopal Church, and religion was part of his family life. Art Bridges, which owns the painting, connects its subject with this background. Its interpretation emphasizes how religious values pass between generations through family relationships. [P1][P2]

Tanner returned to the subject of learning in *The Young Sabot Maker* in 1895. It depicts a boy making wooden shoes while an older craftsman watches. This interest in teaching connects his scenes of African American life with his paintings of rural France. [P5; editorial interpretation]

### Painting limits

- The older man and boy are not identified as grandfather and grandson. Sources qualify that relationship.
- Sharing prayer is visible and documented. Cultural transmission is an attributed reading, not proof of the figures' identities or private thoughts.
- No claim is made that one domestic scene documents the practices of all Black families.
- The final connection in B is an editorial comparison of documented subjects across three works. It does not claim Tanner explained them as a single planned series.
- The comparison avoids treating poverty as a virtue or describing the figures as passive symbols of suffering.

## Book: The Remains of the Day

Kazuo Ishiguro, 1989. Novel. These drafts avoid major ending developments.

### A: More context

Published in 1989, *The Remains of the Day* is a novel by Kazuo Ishiguro. Its central character, Stevens, is a fictional English butler with decades of service at Darlington Hall. In the story's 1956 setting, he takes a driving holiday to visit Miss Kenton, the house's former housekeeper. The journey prompts memories of their work together and of his former employer, Lord Darlington. [B1][B2][B3]

Those memories draw on the real political background of Europe before the Second World War, including the rise of Nazism. Within the novel, the fictional Lord Darlington hosts political meetings and supports closer relations between Britain and Germany. Stevens takes pride in serving him and trusts his judgment. [B3]

Back in the novel's 1956 setting, the house belongs to an American, Mr Farraday. Stevens reports that it now has four resident staff, including himself. He recalls once supervising seventeen employees. His opening account concerns recruitment, work schedules, and maintaining earlier standards with fewer people. [B1]

In a later interview about the novel, Ishiguro explained why he had given Stevens such a limited perspective. He wanted to show how people could follow the assumptions of their time and later regret their part in events. [B3]

### B: More attention to the narration

In the story's 1956 setting, he takes a driving holiday to visit Miss Kenton, the house's former housekeeper. Those memories draw on the real political background of Europe before the Second World War, including the rise of Nazism. Within the novel, the fictional Lord Darlington hosts political meetings and supports closer relations between Britain and Germany. Stevens takes pride in serving him and trusts his judgment. [B3]

Stevens narrates the story himself. In the opening, he says Miss Kenton's letter made him reconsider taking the trip. He briefly asks, “why should I hide it?” Then he insists that she brought “professional matters” to mind. Several paragraphs about staffing follow. He has been making small errors, which he attributes to giving himself too much work. [B1]

Stevens presents his interest in the letter as a matter of work. His careful insistence on professional concerns can also be read as a way of avoiding a more personal explanation. [B1; interpretation of the excerpt]

## Available-reading limitation

These are introductions developed from a publisher-authorized opening excerpt, institutional summaries, and author commentary. The full novel was not read for this experiment. Neither draft claims that it was. The short excerpt supports a local reading of Stevens's explanation, not an exhaustive account of every narrative technique in the book.

The shared context identifies the real prewar political background separately from Lord Darlington's fictional meetings. Both versions omit major ending developments and the outcome of the visit. Stevens's account of household staffing remains a detail of the novel, not historical evidence about British domestic service. The drafts do not treat him as a representative of every British servant.
