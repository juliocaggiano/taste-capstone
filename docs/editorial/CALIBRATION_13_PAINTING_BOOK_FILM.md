## The Third of May 1808 — Francisco Goya, 1814

### A

On 2 May 1808, people in Madrid rose against Napoleon’s occupying troops. French reprisals began that afternoon and continued through the night. Goya painted the executions six years later, alongside a companion painting of the uprising.

In *The Third of May 1808*, unarmed prisoners face a firing squad. One covers his face; another clasps his hands in prayer. The soldiers stand close together with their faces hidden, repeating the same firing position. Goya makes the prisoners’ individual responses visible while presenting the army as a single organized force.

A lantern illuminates the man in white, whose raised arms recall Christ on the cross. This familiar religious pose gives an unnamed victim the importance usually reserved for a martyr. His frightened expression keeps that association close to the experience of someone facing death.

Bodies lie at his feet, and more prisoners approach behind him. The arrangement shows successive stages of the same execution process. Goya commemorates the resistance through the people killed in its suppression, with no victory visible in the scene.

### B

Francisco Goya painted *The Third of May 1808* in 1814, remembering French reprisals after an uprising in occupied Madrid. The painting places us near the condemned men, close enough to distinguish their expressions. The soldiers face away from us, their bodies and rifles arranged in a repeated line.

That difference gives the scene much of its meaning. Military order appears on one side; on the other, people respond separately with fear, prayer, and anger. The man in white spreads his arms, echoing the crucified Christ. Religious imagery gives his death the dignity of martyrdom, although Goya supplies no name or biography that would distinguish him from the other prisoners.

He also prevents this figure from becoming an isolated sacrifice. A dead man lies in the foreground with his arms similarly extended, while further prisoners wait behind the central group. What is about to happen has already happened to others.

The uprising itself appears in Goya’s companion painting, *The Second of May 1808*. Together, the canvases follow popular resistance into its violent suppression. *The Third of May* concentrates on the condemned men’s helplessness once the army has regained control.

## The Great Gatsby — F. Scott Fitzgerald, 1925

### A

F. Scott Fitzgerald’s *The Great Gatsby*, published in 1925, follows a wealthy man trying to recover a relationship from five years earlier. In the novel’s 1922 setting, Gatsby lives across the bay from Daisy Buchanan, who has married into an established fortune.

Born James Gatz to poor farmers, he had begun inventing a different identity before meeting her. Daisy later became central to that ambition. Reuniting with her would confirm that he had become the person he once imagined. His fortune from criminal dealings buys the house and parties that might bring her within reach, but cannot give him the inherited standing of her husband, Tom.

Gatsby also expects Daisy to erase the years between them. He tells Nick, the narrator:

> I’m going to fix everything just the way it was before.

He wants her to say she never loved Tom. Fitzgerald connects this private hope with the American belief that people can remake their lives. Gatsby has changed his name and circumstances, but his plan now depends on another person. Daisy must fit the life he has imagined, including his version of her past.

### B

In F. Scott Fitzgerald’s *The Great Gatsby* (1925), Nick Carraway moves between two wealthy Long Island communities. His neighbour Gatsby has recently acquired a fortune; across the bay, Tom and Daisy Buchanan belong to an established wealthy circle. Their houses suggest similar prosperity, but their money does not carry the same social authority.

Gatsby hopes to recover his former relationship with Daisy. Speaking to Nick, he notices something inseparable from her appeal:

> Her voice is full of money.

His attachment joins a particular woman to the ease and security of the world she inhabits. Born into poverty, Gatsby changed his name and invented a different identity. Criminal business later helped finance his ambitions. These ambitions predate Daisy, but winning her back now promises to confirm that reinvention.

The novel tests the American promise that someone can rise above their origins and build the life they want. Gatsby has acquired the visible signs of success, yet still measures it through Daisy’s response. He even expects her to deny ever having loved her husband. A new relationship would not satisfy him; he wants the old one restored on his terms.

## Guillermo del Toro’s Pinocchio — Guillermo del Toro and Mark Gustafson, 2022

### A

In *Guillermo del Toro’s Pinocchio* (2022), Geppetto loses his son Carlo in a wartime bombing. Years later, still grieving, he carves a wooden boy who is brought to life. The child is curious, noisy, and difficult to control. Geppetto keeps comparing him with Carlo, leaving Pinocchio struggling to please a father who wanted someone else. Accepting him means getting to know a child who differs from the one Geppetto remembers.

Directed by del Toro and Mark Gustafson, the stop-motion film places this troubled family in Fascist Italy. Outside the home, Pinocchio encounters adults who also have a fixed idea of what a boy should become. A local official demands discipline and obedience; children are prepared for military service. In that setting, refusing to obey can mean resisting what adults are teaching them.

His encounters with Death add another difficulty. Pinocchio can return to life, but Geppetto cannot count on the same protection. The father is preoccupied with the child he has lost; Pinocchio gradually has to understand that he could lose his father.

### B

The adults in *Guillermo del Toro’s Pinocchio* (2022) repeatedly try to decide what kind of boy Pinocchio should be. Set in Fascist Italy, the stop-motion film gives obedience a political consequence: children are expected to become disciplined soldiers. The wooden boy’s curiosity and disobedience disturb a society that rewards conformity.

At home, the pressure has a more personal cause. Geppetto carved Pinocchio while grieving for Carlo, his son killed in a wartime bombing. When the puppet comes to life, Geppetto compares him with the child he remembers. Pinocchio wants his affection, but his lively, unpredictable character disappoints those expectations. Loving him requires Geppetto to allow a different child into his life.

Co-directors Guillermo del Toro and Mark Gustafson connect that struggle with the fear of losing someone again. Pinocchio discovers that he can return from death, although the people he loves cannot. His survival offers Geppetto no way to recover Carlo, and gives Pinocchio no guarantee of keeping his father. Even with the fantasy of an immortal child, the time they can spend together remains limited.
