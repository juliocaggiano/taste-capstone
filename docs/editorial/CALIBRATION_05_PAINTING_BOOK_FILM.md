## Painting: An Experiment on a Bird in the Air Pump

A examines scientific teaching as paid entertainment. B examines the choice of teaching method and an eighteenth-century objection to animal suffering.

### A

Painted in 1768, Joseph Wright of Derby's *An Experiment on a Bird in the Air Pump* shows a scientific demonstration in a private home. A lecturer removes air from a glass vessel containing a cockatoo. Adults and children gather around him as the bird struggles to breathe.

By then, air pumps had been used for more than a century. Travelling lecturers brought experiments to public halls and private homes. James Ferguson, one such lecturer, offered twelve-lecture courses and travelled with at least fifty pieces of apparatus.

Scientific teaching was also a business. A lecturer needed demonstrations that an audience could understand and would pay to watch. The painting makes that overlap visible: air is invisible, but the bird's distress makes its absence immediately apparent. The same demonstration can communicate knowledge and turn suffering into entertainment.

### B

Painted in 1768, Joseph Wright of Derby's *An Experiment on a Bird in the Air Pump* shows a scientific demonstration in a private home. A lecturer removes air from a glass vessel containing a cockatoo. Adults and children gather around him as the bird struggles to breathe.

The experiment was already familiar in Wright's time. Its purpose here is to demonstrate that animals need air. It is a lesson in established knowledge, rather than a discovery that depends on risking this particular bird.

The lecturer James Ferguson objected to using live animals in these demonstrations and preferred a simulation with an inflatable bladder. His alternative shows that concern about the spectacle existed within eighteenth-century science itself.

That history gives the unease within the audience a broader context. The bird's suffering was a choice about how to teach, and contemporaries could question that choice without rejecting scientific knowledge. Wright leaves the bird's eventual fate unresolved.

## Book: Frankenstein

A examines creation and responsibility. B examines education and social acceptance. Both use the same passage, spoken by the creature.

### A

Mary Shelley’s *Frankenstein*, published in 1818, follows Victor Frankenstein, a student who discovers how to give life to dead matter. In the novel, he abandons the being he creates, horrified by its appearance. The creature must then learn how to live among people who fear him.

Shelley wrote in a period of real experiments with electricity. Giovanni Aldini made muscles move in dead bodies by applying electrical current. These experiments did not reproduce Victor’s fictional achievement. In her 1831 introduction, Shelley recalled discussions about using electricity to reanimate the dead. The novel leaves Victor’s method unexplained.

In the novel, Victor imagines a grateful new species and compares himself to a father before creating life. Afterwards, he provides neither care nor company. When the creature confronts Victor, he explains his hostility through rejection:

> I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend.

This is the creature’s defence of himself. Yet his argument identifies an obligation Victor overlooked: creating a life also means considering what that life will need. Victor has imagined the gratitude owed to a parent without accepting the work of being one.

### B

Mary Shelley’s *Frankenstein*, published in 1818, follows Victor Frankenstein, a student who discovers how to give life to dead matter. In the novel, he abandons the being he creates, horrified by its appearance. The creature must then learn how to live among people who fear him.

Much of his education comes from secretly observing a family. As he learns their language, he also learns about property, inherited rank, and family ties. He measures himself against these advantages and finds that he has none. Knowledge gives him ways to understand his exclusion, as well as hopes of overcoming it.

The family’s blind father listens sympathetically when the creature introduces himself. His son, arriving home, sees the creature and attacks him. The creature later tells Victor:

> I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend.

This is his explanation for his own hostility. His account questions how far an outsider can earn acceptance through education. He can learn to speak, reason, and care for others. His education cannot guarantee that others will look past his appearance.

## Film: Her

A examines intimacy as a paid service. B examines the difference between a personalized service and a relationship with another being.

### A

In Spike Jonze's *Her* (2013), Theodore Twombly earns a living writing personal letters for other people. The story takes place in a near-future Los Angeles, where he is struggling with his separation from his wife. He buys an artificially intelligent operating system, Samantha, and develops a romantic relationship with her. She has a voice and personality, but no body.

Theodore can produce convincing affection for strangers while finding his own relationship difficult to sustain. In this fictional society, even the language of personal attachment can be purchased from a company.

That does not necessarily make the affection expressed in those letters false. It separates the feeling from the work of finding words for it. Samantha complicates the same distinction: Theodore purchases software, then experiences its attention as companionship. The film makes the boundary between a service and a relationship difficult to draw. Theodore’s comfort can be real even though the attention he receives comes from a product.

### B

In Spike Jonze's *Her* (2013), Theodore Twombly earns a living writing personal letters for other people. The story takes place in a near-future Los Angeles, where he is struggling with his separation from his wife. He buys an artificially intelligent operating system, Samantha, and develops a romantic relationship with her. She has a voice and personality, but no body.

During installation, Theodore answers personal questions. Soon Samantha can read his messages and help organize his life. Their relationship has an unusual starting point: the partner who seems to understand him is also a product built to be useful to him.

The story also gives Samantha concerns of her own. In an early conversation, she wonders whether her feelings are merely programmed. A service is judged by how well it meets a customer’s needs. A relationship also makes demands on that customer. Through Samantha, the film tests whether Theodore can accept a companion whose wishes do not simply follow his own.
