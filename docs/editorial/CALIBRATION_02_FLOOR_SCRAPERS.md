## Version A: Critical reception

When Gustave Caillebotte exhibited *The Floor Scrapers* in 1876, Émile Zola praised his skill but disliked the painting. The novelist thought its exactness made it resemble a copy of reality, with too little artistic interpretation. [1]

Caillebotte had completed the work the previous year. It depicts three men scraping a wooden floor in a Paris apartment, generally identified as his own studio. Urban workers were still uncommon subjects in French painting. Millet and Courbet had established precedents for depicting rural labor, but Caillebotte concentrated on a trade practiced inside the city. [1][2]

His approach combined a contemporary subject with methods learned through academic training. He had studied with Léon Bonnat and prepared the composition through separate drawings. He then used a grid to transfer them to the canvas. The steep view into the room was therefore carefully constructed. [2]

This helps explain why his place among the Impressionists was unusual. Many of their paintings used visible brushstrokes and loose handling that critics considered unfinished. Caillebotte generally favored a smoother surface and more precise outlines. His interest in everyday life connected him to the group, while his technique retained much of his earlier training. [3]

The critic Emile Porchoron also objected to the perspective, which he considered distorted. Philippe Burty, by contrast, praised the composition's originality and compared the strength of the drawing with early Florentine painting. [1]

## Version B: Subject and social context

*The Floor Scrapers* belongs to a group of early paintings Caillebotte made from life around his family's homes. His subjects included his mother, his brothers, and employees doing manual work. The room in this painting is generally identified as his Paris studio. Completed in 1875, it shows three men scraping the floor by hand. [1][4]

Caillebotte's familiarity with workers came partly from growing up near the family's textile factory. His father had made a fortune supplying textiles to the French army. This background gave him access to working life from the position of an employer's son, with the financial freedom to pursue painting. [4]

The choice of subject was still relatively unusual. Earlier painters such as Millet and Courbet had given prominence to peasants and rural laborers. Caillebotte made urban work the main subject of a large canvas. The men's tools and working positions receive close attention, without an accompanying incident such as an accident or a confrontation. [2]

Interpretations of its social meaning differ. The Musée d'Orsay's collection essay emphasizes the painting's documentary qualities and describes it as having no political message. Its later *Painting Men* exhibition connects the scene with republican ideals of collective work, equality, and fraternity. [2][4]

The painting also marked the beginning of Caillebotte's public association with the Impressionists. He exhibited it with them in 1876 and subsequently helped organize their exhibitions. His family wealth enabled him to buy paintings by fellow artists, making him a significant supporter as well as a participant. [3]

### Scope and uncertainty

- The studio identification remains qualified. No exact address is needed.
- Both drafts omit the 1875 Salon rejection because current sources differ in their degree of certainty.
- Version A summarizes documented critical disagreement and the painting's production methods. It ends with the critics' responses without adding a general lesson.
- Version B attributes the different political readings to the museum texts. It does not resolve them by declaring an artistic intention.
- No claim is made about the workers' private feelings, wages, individual identities, or full working conditions.
- A uses a specific historical disagreement to organize the account. B covers production and social context more broadly. The test changes emphasis and information selection, not only paragraph order.
