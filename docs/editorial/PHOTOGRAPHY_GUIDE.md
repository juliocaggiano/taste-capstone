# Taste photography writing guide

Version 0.2 · 26 September 2026 · Photography selection refined by Julio.

Use this supplement with [VOICE_GUIDE.md](VOICE_GUIDE.md). The shared guide contains the full writing calibration. This file adapts it to individual photographs; it does not replace that feedback.

## Purpose

Use a specific photograph to explain something meaningful about people's lives, a place, a practice or an event. Give the reader enough context to understand what is happening and why it matters. A photograph's formal achievement, humor or ambiguity can also be the main concern. Do not turn every image into a story about suffering.

The entry's subject is the photograph. A photographer's series supplies context; their biography belongs only where it explains the image or access to its subject. A caption and a career summary alone are insufficient.

## What the Notion studies contribute

The research starts from the sixteen profiles captured from The Grace of Pascal on 19 September. The Lawrence, Salgado, Sternfeld, Seymour and Addario pages were reopened on 25 September. These pages mix collected captions, criticism, quotations and biographical writing. Their inclusion establishes an editorial interest, not authorship or independent verification of every claim.

- **Lawrence and Seymour:** names, places, dates and circumstances keep people from becoming anonymous illustrations of a theme.
- **Salgado:** explain work and material conditions alongside the people shown. Retain the series' context without assuming every photograph expresses its entire argument.
- **Sternfeld:** a small, ordinary detail can support humor and a social observation. A surprising image also needs its actual circumstances.
- **Addario:** access to a setting can reveal a neglected story. Keep the photographed person's experience central; the photographer's bravery is not a substitute for that account.
- **Gruyaert and Crewdson:** distinguish the pleasure of color and everyday observation from a deliberately constructed fictional scene. Do not apply documentary assumptions to staged work.
- **Demir:** circulation and public response may be part of a photograph's history. Verify claimed consequences rather than assuming that attention changed policy.

## Research the photograph and the subject

1. **Fix the exact image.** Record the photographer, title or descriptive caption, exposure date, place, sitter names where documented, series and source record. Separate the exposure date from publication and print dates. A neighboring frame from the same event is another photograph.
2. **Inspect the original.** Record the visible actions, objects and relationships that matter. Check the edges as well as the central subject. Do not infer identity, belief, distress or a medical condition from appearance.
3. **Read its circumstances.** Consult the photographer's caption or testimony and a substantial museum, journalistic or scholarly account. Establish whether the image was commissioned, posed, observed, staged or made for advertising when that changes its meaning.
4. **Research the represented world.** Look for local archives, relevant scholarship and accounts from people within the community. A photographer can establish how a portrait was made without being the sole authority on a people's customs or history.
5. **Challenge the first reading.** Look for changed captions, alternative accounts, related frames and reasoned public discussion. Reader reactions can expose a misleading impression; they cannot establish what happened. Trace consequential claims to evidence.

Keep four evidence types separate in research notes: visible detail, documented circumstance, attributed interpretation and editorial inference. Identify the source and its date. Do not resolve a disputed date or identity by counting how often it appears online.

## Develop one connected account

Start with the person, event or activity and its setting. Explain what a first-time reader needs before naming a wider social concept. Introduce unfamiliar institutions, beliefs and terms close to the detail they clarify.

Choose the photograph's central connection before drafting. For example, a hospital journey can explain how distance and transport shape access to care. A mining scene can explain the relationship between physical work, ownership and the hope of earning money. Listing clothing, landscape and facial expressions would miss those connections.

Use a few visible details as evidence. Explain how they make the account understandable. Then add the history or cultural knowledge that the image cannot provide on its own. A useful caption fact may overturn the apparent story; introduce it early enough to prevent a false reading.

For cultural practices, explain who practices them, in which setting, and what participants or researchers say they mean. Include variation and disagreement where they materially change the account. Pride, social pressure, economic incentives and personal preference may coexist. Do not give every person in a community one motive or treat a living society as outside modern history.

Distinguish what happened during the exposure from what happened afterward. A later interview may supply an outcome. Do not write that outcome as if it were visible in the photograph.

Write in clear, restrained prose for a high school reader. Keep the seriousness of events without dramatic rhetoric. Avoid exoticizing language, guessed inner monologues, a descriptive inventory, and a generic final sentence about humanity. End with the last useful explanation. No fixed paragraph count is required.

Do not append a routine warning that photographs are interpretive. Explain the specific missing context when it matters. Research-based cultural analysis is appropriate; do not claim to have conducted ethnographic fieldwork or interviewed the subject.

## Use testimony when it adds something

A short passage from a sitter, photographer or contemporary account can enrich the story. Choose it for what the reader learns, not because it is a memorable slogan. Verify the exact words and surrounding context. Identify the speaker naturally before the passage. A quotation from another member of the community is not the sitter's testimony.

Retain full attribution, translation, date, locator and usage limits in the source record. Keep redundant credit lines and research-process commentary outside the reading text, following the shared guide.

## Images and editorial decisions

Use an authentic source image with the full published composition. Preserve its color, tonal treatment, grain and recorded damage. Do not generate replacement people, remove objects, invent detail, replace backgrounds or synthesize another exposure. A source's published crop should be identified as such; never silently present it as the complete frame.

Julio's explicit decision on 25 September: **“One original image; avoid artificial variations.”** Where only one faithful source is available, show that original alone. Offer additional reproductions only when genuine source differences give him a useful choice. Do not create three copies, crops or color treatments to meet a numerical quota.

September 26 refinement: distinct photographs by the same photographer of the same subject may be offered as image choices. These must be separate authentic exposures, each with its own caption, date and source. Write shared context only when it applies to every option; keep frame-specific observations attached to that option. Selecting a different photograph must trigger a consistency check of the text before approval. Never invent alternate frames or recolor a photograph.

Julio wants to retain the Serra Pelada, McLean, Virginia and Noor Nisa proposals. He clarified that “McCully” meant Joel Sternfeld’s McLean, Virginia. This is a selection preference, not approval of their writing or images. Replace the Engalo and Chim proposals with new subjects while retaining their files and review history.

Record the source URL, credit, pixel dimensions, original asset and any known reproduction differences. Store public-use clearance separately from editorial approval. A single image still requires Julio's explicit selection; do not auto-approve it. Writing, image selection and passage comments remain independent review controls.

## Final review

- Does the opening identify the specific photograph and orient an unfamiliar reader?
- Can the reader explain the central social, historical, cultural or visual connection after reading the actual prose?
- Are its most important claims supported beyond appearance and the photographer's general biography?
- Are the supplied image, caption and account about the same exposure?
- Have names, dates, staged circumstances and materially conflicting testimony been checked?
- Does a community appear through specific practices and people rather than a stereotype?
- Does every paragraph add a useful explanation, without repetitive conclusions or dramatic overstatement?

These checks establish a draft's readiness for review. Julio's decisions on the five first entries will calibrate this supplement further.
