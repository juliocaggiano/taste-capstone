## Version A: More context

*The Remains of the Day* follows Stevens, a butler who has spent decades at Darlington Hall in England. In 1956, he takes a driving holiday to visit Miss Kenton, its former housekeeper. The journey prompts memories of their work together and of his former employer, Lord Darlington. [1][2][3]

Much of that past belongs to the years before the Second World War. Darlington involved himself in relations between Britain and Germany, hosting political meetings at his house. Stevens takes pride in serving him and trusts his judgment. [3]

By the time Stevens sets out, the house belongs to an American, Mr Farraday. Its permanent staff has fallen to four, including Stevens. He recalls once supervising seventeen employees. His opening account is occupied with schedules, recruitment, and the difficulty of running the house with fewer people. [1]

He is still trying to maintain the standards associated with his earlier career, while the household operates differently. Ishiguro later explained that Stevens's limited perspective mattered to the novel. He wanted to show how people could follow the assumptions of their time and subsequently regret their part in events. [1][3]

## Version B: More attention to the narration

*The Remains of the Day* follows Stevens, a butler who has spent decades at Darlington Hall in England. In 1956, he takes a driving holiday to visit Miss Kenton, its former housekeeper. The journey prompts memories of their work together and of his former employer, Lord Darlington. [1][2][3]

Much of that past belongs to the years before the Second World War. Darlington involved himself in relations between Britain and Germany, hosting political meetings at his house. Stevens takes pride in serving him and trusts his judgment. [3]

In the opening, Stevens says Miss Kenton's letter made him reconsider taking the trip. He briefly asks, “why should I hide it?” Then he insists that she brought “professional matters” to mind. Several paragraphs about staffing follow. He has been making small errors, which he attributes to giving himself too much work. [1]

That correction makes his insistence on a purely professional motive open to doubt. He volunteers an explanation before anyone has challenged him. The staffing problem is real within the story, so his professional motive cannot simply be dismissed. What the passage leaves open is whether that motive explains his whole interest in the visit. ## Available-reading limitation

These are introductions developed from a publisher-authorized opening excerpt, institutional summaries, and author commentary. The full novel was not read for this experiment. Neither draft claims that it was. The short excerpt supports a local reading of Stevens's explanation, not an exhaustive account of every narrative technique in the book.

The shared context includes the prewar political setting. Both versions omit major ending developments and the outcome of the visit. The drafts do not treat Stevens as a representative of every British servant or use the fictional household as statistical evidence about domestic service.
