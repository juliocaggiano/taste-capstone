## Analysis before revision

- Central account: political appointments and abandonment created a public scandal. The stranded group also experienced interpersonal violence, killings and survival cannibalism. Leaving those out reduced the painting to victims seeking rescue and erased a central complication in its human subject.
- Human experience: desperation to stay alive, violence against companions, exhaustion, and the continuing effort to secure help. Do not infer that every painted individual committed the acts or assign identical motives to all occupants.
- Development: explain the grounding and abandonment, what happened during the drift, then the specific moment Géricault chose. The finished canvas shows bodies and signals toward a distant ship; it does not directly depict cannibalism or killings.
- Cultural/formal connection: restored monarchy and royal patronage; heroic-scale history painting used for an ordeal that included both institutional abandonment and violence among those abandoned.

## Revised entry

In July 1816, the French frigate Méduse ran aground off West Africa while carrying officials and settlers to Senegal. Its captain had obtained his command through royal connections despite having spent twenty years away from the sea. When the ship could not be freed, the captain and governor left in boats.

About 150 people were placed on a makeshift raft, which was cut loose from the boats towing it. Alongside hunger and thirst, violence broke out among its occupants. Some resorted to cannibalism, eating the bodies of the dead. Living companions were also thrown overboard. Only fifteen people remained alive when rescue came almost two weeks later. Their accounts made both the abandonment and the events on the raft part of a scandal for France’s restored monarchy.

Géricault met survivors while preparing the painting. He shows the later attempt to attract a distant ship, with dead and exhausted bodies beneath the men signalling for help. A Black man waving a cloth stands at the top of the group. The effort to secure rescue follows days in which some of those stranded had harmed one another to stay alive.

Exhibited in 1819, the painting gave this ordeal the monumental scale and carefully arranged bodies associated with heroic history painting.
